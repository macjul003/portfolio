## The problem

The promise was clear: AI agents that handle crypto options trading on your behalf, without needing to understand the underlying mechanics. The reality users encountered was something different — opaque agent cards full of jargon, no guidance on how to choose between them, and no visibility into what happened after depositing funds.

The gap between promise and reality was where adoption was dying. Users loved the idea. They left before the first trade.

The clearest signal came from a single user quote during research: *"If I don't understand the agent, I can't trust it with my money."* That sentence became the design brief.

## The user

Jason is 27, comfortable with staking and swapping, new to options. He wants to grow his money without learning options strategy from scratch. His mental model isn't "I want to execute sophisticated trades" — it's "I want a simple, safe way to earn, and I need to believe it won't blow up my portfolio."

He's not the only user, but he's the most representative. The product's ambition was accessibility. Jason was the test of whether we'd delivered it.

## The redesign

The initial work was a direct response to the usability failures: confusing agent cards rewritten in plain language, a clear selection flow with context about each agent's strategy and risk profile, and post-deposit transparency so users could see what was happening to their funds.

Before/after was stark. The original cards looked like Bloomberg terminal output — dense, technical, designed for someone who already knew what they were doing. The redesigned version looked like something you could hand to Jason and he'd understand within thirty seconds.

Ran Maze tests on the new onboarding flow. Tracked every click, every drop-off point, refined tooltips and modals based on what the data showed. The numbers improved. But something else came out of the testing that changed the direction of the project entirely.

## The pivot

The redesign fixed usability. It didn't fix trust.

Users understood the agents better. They still hesitated to deposit. The barrier wasn't comprehension anymore — it was the cold logic of putting real money into a product they'd just met. No history with it. No community around it. No sense that other people were doing this and it was working.

The insight that shifted the product direction: converting onboarding friction into engagement through a game. Not gamification as decoration — a game as a behavioral engine that built the pre-qualified, motivated user base the product needed.

## The game

Users connect their wallet and receive five random tokens from a curated pool of twenty-five. Each token is named after a famous lake. Token prices update in real-time, simulating a mini-market. To qualify for early access, users need to collect a specific set of tokens.

The only way to complete the set: trade, strategize, and engage with other players. Airdrops every three hours bring new chances. Alliances form. Negotiations happen. The game creates the exact conditions — competition, progression, community — that make people come back without being asked.

The result wasn't just engagement. It was pre-qualification. By the time a user completed the game and accessed the real product, they had already demonstrated they could think in trading terms and they wanted to be there.

## What happened

Within days of launch, Discord lit up. Users built custom spreadsheets for token bartering. Some wrote bots to track airdrops. New strategies emerged organically. When the Mission Hub and a Battleship mini-game launched alongside the core loop, engagement jumped again.

Everything was tracked. A/B tests on drop timing, Maze tests on onboarding, continuous refinement of every tooltip and animation based on real behaviour. The game was iterated the same way a product is iterated — with data, not instinct.

## What we learned

Games aren't fluff. When done right, they become behavioral engines — systems that reward curiosity, strategy, and community in ways that pure product onboarding can't. People didn't just use the product. They played with it, talked about it, brought friends in.

The deeper lesson is about trust. No amount of cleaner UI closes the gap between "I understand this" and "I'm willing to put money into this." That gap requires time and experience — or a game that compresses both into something people genuinely want to do.

When you design for play, you unlock something that traditional onboarding can't: not just usage, but obsession.
